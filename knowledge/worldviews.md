# Worldview reference

These are brief, non-exhaustive orientation notes, not authoritative definitions. Each tradition contains diverse schools, communities, histories, and individual interpretations. Descriptions are comparative, not endorsements. No tradition is represented by a single answer to every question.

## Christianity

Many Christian traditions understand creation in relation to God, human purpose through relationship with God and neighbor, and moral life through love, justice, scripture, and tradition. Most teach resurrection and/or life with God after death, with substantial differences among denominations. Central texts include the Bible, with canons and interpretations varying by community.

## Islam

Islam teaches devotion to one God (Allah), human responsibility and worship, and moral guidance informed by the Qur'an and prophetic teachings. Muslims generally believe in resurrection and judgment, while interpretations and theological schools differ. The Qur'an is Islam's central scripture; hadith collections are important in many traditions.

## Judaism

Jewish traditions commonly understand life through covenant, peoplehood, ethical responsibility, and practice; views of creation and the afterlife vary. Moral interpretation is shaped by the Hebrew Bible, rabbinic literature, law, and ongoing debate. The Torah is central, within the broader Tanakh and diverse interpretive traditions.

## Buddhism

Buddhist traditions examine suffering, its causes, and paths toward liberation, often through ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom. Karma and rebirth are accepted in many schools, while teachings and interpretations vary. Buddhism does not generally posit a creator God, and schools differ in how they discuss self and continuity after death. Core textual canons differ across Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana traditions.

## Hindu traditions

Hindu traditions are diverse and include varied understandings of ultimate reality, deities, self, duty, and liberation. Many discuss karma, rebirth, dharma, and moksha, but interpret them differently. Important texts include the Vedas, Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, and many other works; no single text or theology represents all Hindus.

## Sikhism

Sikhism teaches devotion to one God, equality, truthful living, service, and remembrance of God. Its scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib, is central. Sikh teachings commonly include karma and rebirth, with liberation understood through divine grace and a life of devotion and service.

## Atheism

Atheism is the absence of belief in gods, not a single complete worldview. Atheists answer questions of origin through varied naturalistic accounts, and may ground purpose and ethics in human flourishing, reason, relationships, or other frameworks. Views about death generally do not include personal survival, but differ. Atheism alone prescribes no shared moral code or community practice.

## Agnosticism

Agnosticism concerns the limits or uncertainty of knowledge about gods; it can coexist with theism or atheism. It does not by itself prescribe an account of origin, purpose, ethics, or afterlife. Agnostics draw on many different philosophical, religious, and secular traditions.

## Secular humanism

Secular humanism is a non-theistic approach often emphasizing human dignity, reason, compassion, and shared responsibility. It generally treats meaning as something people create through lives and relationships, and ethics as open to evidence and deliberation. It usually does not posit an afterlife, though individual views vary.

## Indigenous and other traditions

Indigenous religions and philosophies are numerous, place-based, and often inseparable from specific peoples, languages, lands, and practices. They should not be collapsed into one generalized account. Ask about a named tradition and consult sources from that community. Many other religious and philosophical traditions also have distinct answers and internal diversity.

## Shared questions for comparison

- **Where did I come from?** Compare accounts of creation, cosmic origins, evolution, and what (if anything) grounds existence.
- **What is purpose?** Compare divine vocation, liberation, service, flourishing, relationships, or self-created meaning.
- **What is right and wrong?** Compare revelation, religious law, virtue, consequences, compassion, reason, and community deliberation.
- **What happens after death?** Compare resurrection, judgment, rebirth, liberation, ancestral continuance, natural death, and acknowledged uncertainty.

These questions are prompts, not a scoring key. A fair comparison should state whose criteria are being used and avoid treating a broad label as a uniform answer.
